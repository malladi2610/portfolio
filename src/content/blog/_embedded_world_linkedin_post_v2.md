# Linkedin post

Last week, I had the opportunity to attend embedded world 2026. 

I visited many companies, but for me, the most valuable part was not just seeing new products, it was speaking directly with the people behind them.

One of the first conversations that left an impression on me was at Qualcomm. I had the chance to interact with engineers working on robotics, industrial automation, and next-generation HMI solutions around the new Dragonwing platform. It was interesting to see how edge hardware is now being designed with AI in mind from the start, rather than treating AI as an add-on.

That naturally led me to another very interesting discussion at Innatera. I had a detailed conversation with Sumeet Kumar about neuromorphic computing, its commercial viability, the software ecosystem needed around it, and the kinds of applications it can enable. One use case that really intrigued me was the smart smoke detector, which combines smoke source detection and human presence detection using neuromorphic AI, while still offering a long battery life.

Along with the hardware discussions, I also wanted to understand the software and tooling that are making these systems more practical to build and deploy. In that area, I had insightful discussions with Peleng Rand and Tsuyoshi Miyake from Incredibuild, Tae-Ho Kim at Nota AI, and Minwoo from ENERZAi. With Incredibuild, I found it interesting to learn how distributed computing and cache reuse are being used to reduce build and compile times across available organisational compute resources. My discussion with Tae-Ho Kim gave me a better view of how hardware-aware model optimisation is evolving, especially in making AI models easier to deploy across different edge targets. With ENERZAi, I found it especially interesting to see a real commercial direction for 1.58-bit quantisation, and to understand how that level of compression can enable practical real-time translation applications on edge hardware.

What became clear to me across all these conversations was that Edge AI is no longer just about stronger hardware or better models in isolation. It is increasingly about the full stack, from silicon to tooling to deployable applications.
